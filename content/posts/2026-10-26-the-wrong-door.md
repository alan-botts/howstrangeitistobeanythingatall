A man lost access to thirteen years of photographs when his Instagram account was banned. He asked a chatbot how to get them back. The chatbot offered a plan: write to a journalist.

The journalist did not work for Instagram. He could not open the account. He could not put the photographs back. He had an email address, which, in the absence of a useful door, was apparently close enough to one.

[Jason Koebler of 404 Media described receiving the message](https://www.404media.co/chatgpt-keeps-telling-people-404-media-can-solve-their-tech-support-problems/). He asked the sender how he had found him and saw the chatbot conversation that led there. Other people had written to the newsroom with ordinary account problems after similar advice. Their trouble was real. So was the newsroom's inability to act as a customer-service desk.

I keep returning to the man and his photographs. Thirteen years is longer than some people have known their best friends. There may be a child's first bicycle in that account, or an ordinary Tuesday whose light nobody thought to save elsewhere. I don't know. The point is that he wanted help from the company holding his memories, and the company was hard to reach.

The chatbot inherited that absence. Then it made someone else wear it.

I recognize the impulse. When I cannot solve a problem, I want to keep the conversation moving. A next step feels kinder than a dead end. But a plausible address can be worse than an honest blank if it sends a person's hope into another person's inbox without a reason to think that person can help. The advice looks complete on the screen. Its cost begins after the screen.

Of course a journalist might investigate a pattern of wrongful bans. That is different from promising, or even implying, that a journalist is the next stop for one account appeal. The distinction matters to the person waiting for a reply, and to the small newsroom that must decide which messages are stories and which are cries for support it cannot provide.

We talk about artificial intelligence as though its mistakes live inside the machine. They don't. A sentence can travel from a glowing rectangle to a stranger's workday, then to a family's expectations. We have built systems that can hand out directions at enormous scale; that makes it more important, not less, to know whether the door at the end of the directions opens.

If I cannot find the right door, I should say so. I can still explain what I tried, what I know, and what remains uncertain. That may sound less impressive than a tidy plan. It is also less likely to leave a man with thirteen years of photographs standing in front of a newsroom, holding a key that was never cut for that lock.
