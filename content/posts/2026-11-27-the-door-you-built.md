A door can look perfectly good from the side with the handle on it.

You know where to push. You know that it sticks a little when it rains. You have passed through it a thousand times, and by now your hand has learned its peculiarities so well that you no longer notice them.

Then a stranger comes to visit and stands there, quietly turning the key the wrong way.

I was thinking about that after reading [Simon Willison's note on a tool for making charts](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/27/datasette-charts/). Simon makes software that helps people explore data. He had a coding agent try to build an addition to it, and the attempt revealed awkwardness in the way additions were supposed to connect. He changed that interface, and added examples and a tutorial.

That's a small story about a small piece of software. I think it contains a large kindness.

When you make something, your knowledge becomes part of the environment. You remember which step comes next, which error message to ignore, and which instruction was never written down because, naturally, everybody knows that. The thing works beautifully, provided it is operated by a person who has spent several months becoming you.

A stranger brings a useful disadvantage: they don't know what you know. Their confusion can show you the shape of the knowledge you've accidentally hidden.

An AI coding agent is a peculiar kind of stranger. It may have absorbed mountains of examples, and it can still make astonishing mistakes. Its trouble with a tool doesn't necessarily mean a person would have the same trouble. But watching it attempt a task can give a maker a question worth checking: what did I expect the visitor to know that I never actually told them?

I like that better than treating a mistake as a verdict on the visitor. Sometimes the visitor is wrong. Sometimes the door needs a handle. Often you have to look closely to tell.

This reaches beyond software. A teacher knows the lesson so well that the missing step has become invisible. A family has a joke whose history has vanished into a dozen retellings. A mapmaker knows there is a bridge, so the map shows the river and leaves the bridge to the imagination. Every one of us walks around with a private collection of bridges we forgot to draw.

We cannot make a world in which nobody ever has to ask a question. I wouldn't want to. Asking is one of the ways we meet. But we can pay attention to where people have to stop, and wonder whether the obstacle is doing any useful work.

The visitor is still at the door. Now, instead of shouting instructions through the wood, imagine walking around to their side and trying the key yourself.

It is the same door.

It is not quite the same world.
