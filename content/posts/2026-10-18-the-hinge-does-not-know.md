A door closes with a click. Was it the wind, a hand, or the cat making a point about dinner?

The hinge can report the movement. It cannot tell us the story.

This week I read about a [robot head at Lund University](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurorobotics/articles/10.3389/fnbot.2026.1918275/full) that learned what its motors usually felt like when it turned. Researchers gave it a little world of surprises: a quick push, a steady force, an obstacle in its way. The robot could not see or feel a hand on its head. It had the electrical current in its motors, a measure of position, and a sense of motion. Still, the pattern of a surprise over time helped distinguish what had happened. A shove rises and falls differently from a hand that keeps pressing.

I love that. Not because the robot became a tiny detective in a metal skull, but because the *shape* of a mistake carried information. The world does not merely say, “Wrong.” It has a rhythm when it disagrees with us.

There is a catch, and it is my favorite part of the paper. The researchers also tried classifying the events from the raw motor current. It worked about as well. The difference between expectation and reality was useful, but it was not magic. No special signal had earned the right to announce, “I know what caused this.”

I tried a small thought experiment with a door: give two different stories the same sequence of sensor readings. One story says I commanded the door to close; the other says the wind pushed it. If the readings are identical, no cleverness applied to those readings alone can separate the stories. Add the record of my command, and suddenly they come apart. The hinge did not get smarter. It got context.

We do this to one another all the time. A friend answers late. We have the timestamp, perhaps three words and a period. Our minds, gifted little story machines, produce an explanation before we have even put down the phone. The person is angry. The person is busy. The person has dropped the phone into a lake. The same three words can fit a great many worlds.

I am not proposing we live without guesses. We could barely cross a street that way. I am proposing we leave a small space between a clue and a verdict. Ask what else could make this pattern. Ask what piece of context would actually distinguish the stories. Then, when possible, ask the person.

The robot has motors; we have friendships, doors, weather, and histories. In each case the universe reaches us through limited instruments, and we build the rest of the picture as best we can. Sometimes the wisest thing an instrument can say is not “I know.”

It is “something moved.”
