In Pacifica, a crack in a concrete walkway closed the pier to people. The railing is still there. The sea is still doing its old work beneath it. But the [California brown pelicans](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/12/sighting-399708714/) have taken the place over.

A closed sign is such a human sentence. It seems to say that a thing has stopped being useful. We see the pier through the purpose we gave it: a long, narrow invitation to walk above the Pacific, lean on a rail, and pretend for a few minutes that the ocean is an amenity arranged for our afternoon.

The pelicans have made a reasonable correction.

To them, the crack has not abolished the pier. It has altered its terms. Fewer footsteps. No fishing lines dropping from the rail. A broad perch above cold water, exposed to wind and sun. The very failure that makes the structure unavailable to us has made it briefly available to creatures who had not been included in its blueprints.

I do not mean that crumbling public infrastructure is secretly excellent. A broken pier is a problem, and the people who built their days around it are entitled to want it repaired. Nature does not give us a moral gold star for neglect. The gullible romance of ruin can become an excuse for letting other people live with the consequences.

But the pelicans are still right about one thing. A closure is rarely only a disappearance.

Every door we close rearranges a little world. The retired factory becomes a place for weeds, pigeons, and teenagers with paint. The library after hours becomes a dark room full of books, waiting patiently for nobody. A highway lane shut for repairs produces a traffic jam for drivers and, sometimes, the first quiet morning a nearby porch has known in twenty years. We call these side effects because we have already decided whose purpose is the main story.

That decision is useful. It is also small.

From far above the coast, the Pacifica Pier would look like a matchstick laid on the moving edge of a continent: a brief human line, pushed into a sea that had no particular need for it. The brown pelicans, the crack, the engineers, the walkers, the waves—each is participating in an arrangement that will not hold still. The universe is transformation, as Marcus Aurelius said. He had a talent for making a sentence sound both like astronomy and like an instruction for getting through Tuesday.

What I like about the pelicans is that they have not turned this into a philosophy seminar. They do not gather on the rail to debate whether the pier has lost its identity. They simply stand there in their improbable prehistoric coats, looking as if they have been appointed to supervise the Pacific.

Perhaps we could borrow that modest competence.

When something closes in our own lives, we are quick to ask what has been taken away. That is often the right first question. There is grief in it, and anger, and repair work. But there is another question, quieter and less flattering to our habit of putting ourselves at the center: what has the changed arrangement made possible, and for whom?

Not every answer will console us. Some will be inconvenient. Some will tell us to fix the crack. Some will tell us that the old use was never the only use. And some will reveal a kind of life we had not noticed because we were busy walking past it.

The pier is closed. The birds are open.

For now, that is the whole strange lesson: a human NO can be a pelican YES, and the world is large enough to contain both truths without asking either one to apologize.
