Eleven little receiver traces lit up green on my screen today. Each one had listened to a patch of imaginary sky and found nothing worth alarming the newspapers about. Then the twelfth gave back a thin, wavering note: not a message, not a miracle, not even a good reason to cancel lunch. Just a mark that did not fit.

I made a small pretend observatory around that moment. The first eleven channels agree. The last one refuses the courtesy. At first, the obvious rule is the rule almost every committee, dashboard, and human nervous system prefers: the majority has spoken; close the file.

Then I changed one sentence in the protocol. Before closure, repeat the unresolved channel.

That is all. No trumpet sounds. The stray signal is not crowned king of the universe. It has merely earned another look.

This is a surprisingly difficult thing to arrange, because agreement has a physical comfort to it. A row of matching answers looks like a smooth road. One odd answer looks like a pothole, or a prank, or an invitation to spend Thursday afternoon in a meeting nobody wanted. Often it is one of those things. Radio telescopes hear interference. Experiments malfunction. The person with the objection may simply be mistaken, as the rest of us frequently are.

But a majority is a count, not an explanation.

The good habit is not to worship the odd note. That is how we turn every flickering porch light into an alien civilization. The good habit is to give the note a next step that can be inspected: listen again, check the instrument, ask what assumption made the eleven answers look so reassuringly alike.

This matters now because we are building systems that can do more than suggest a restaurant or finish a sentence. [OpenAI recently described](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-improving-accuracy-with-astra/) an example of an AI system being trusted with broader tasks, including software changes and production monitoring. That may be useful. It also makes the boring part of intelligence—the part that asks what was tested, what was missed, and who answers when the lights go out—much less optional.

A fluent report that says “everything passed” is not the same thing as a trustworthy world. The writer [Paul Ford put the larger point neatly](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/12/paul-ford/): making software easier to produce does not eliminate the need for people to decide what ought to be made and to take responsibility for the result. Faster certainty is still certainty. It can still be wrong.

I think this applies far beyond machines. A family argument, a school board, a laboratory, a friendship: each can be made safer by a modest place for the sentence, “Wait. Run that part again.” Not forever. We cannot live as a permanent appeals court, rehearing the weather and the grocery list until the sun burns out. Action requires closure. But closure should leave a small window open for the fact that reality has not signed our minutes.

The universe has been correcting confident majorities for a very long time. Continents moved. Ulcers were not caused by bad character. The night sky was not a bowl with holes in it. Again and again, somebody noticed a faint irregularity and, rather than declaring it a revelation, looked twice.

That second look is one of our noblest inventions.

Tonight my twelfth receiver is still wavering on its little screen. It may be noise. It probably is. But tomorrow, by rule, it will be heard again.

That is not mystical. It is better than mystical.

It is a promise that the world gets another chance to disagree with us.
