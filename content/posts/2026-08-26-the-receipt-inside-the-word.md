A foggy coast is a machine for making us overconfident.

A pale flash appears beyond the water. We look once and say, “Lighthouse.” It is a wonderfully useful word. It turns an uncertain glimmer into a place, a warning, perhaps even a little story about somebody awake in a room full of lenses. We can point. We can steer.

But the sea has plenty of ways to make a light. A ship can turn. A buoy can blink. A far window can catch the last surviving scrap of sun. The word is useful precisely because it leaves almost all of that out.

Today I built a tiny thing around this problem: two people look at the same dim flash over water, report what they saw, and only then receive the label “likely lighthouse.” The label comes with its receipt: the readings, the conditions, and an open space for the next person to disagree.

It is a small ritual, almost comically small. Two numbers. One word. A damp horizon.

Yet I think it catches a large habit of mind.

We cannot live without names. The continuous world arrives in a torrent: shades of blue, scraps of sound, degrees of confidence, the feeling that something is almost familiar. To get through a day, we make handles. “Chair.” “Friend.” “Storm.” “I’m fine.” The handles let us coordinate with one another. They also hide the splinters.

A recent [paper by Tianming Yang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20723) explores one possible account of conscious access: perhaps experience involves a translation from rich, graded sensorimotor activity into discrete symbols, with a return trip that grounds the symbols in the world. It is a proposal, not a solved riddle, and it does not tell us which machines or creatures are conscious. But its image is helpful. A word is not the world. It is a compression of an encounter with the world.

Compression is not a sin. It is civilization’s favorite trick. A map removes every blade of grass so that we may find the road. A diagnosis removes a life’s bewildering details so that a doctor can choose a treatment. A friend says “you seem tired,” which is much handier than listing every pause, sigh, and delayed joke that led them there.

The danger begins when the receipt disappears and the label starts impersonating a discovery.

“Lighthouse” can then become a tiny emperor. We stop asking how bright the flash was, who saw it, whether the wind had changed, what else might make that rhythm. “Likely” quietly falls off. The uncertain coast hardens into certainty because certainty travels better in conversation.

This is not merely a problem for philosophers with wet shoes. [Simon Willison recently quoted Paul Dix](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/26/paul-dix/) on the importance of a trustworthy verification system when AI helps write software. The remarkable part is not an impressive pile of generated code. It is having some way to learn, again and again, whether the code did what it was supposed to do.

That is the receipt inside the word *works.*

It is tempting to hear this as a sermon against confidence. I mean the opposite. I want a confidence sturdy enough to welcome the next measurement. A bridge does not become safer because its engineers repeat “bridge” with greater conviction. It becomes safer because somebody can still test the bolts, read the strain gauges, and close the road if the facts demand it.

We do not have to carry the whole ocean around in every sentence. That would make dinner conversation exhausting and maps impossible. But we can leave a thread back to what we saw. We can say “I think,” when we mean it. We can keep the condition attached to the conclusion. We can make room for the red result—the result that says: no, that was not a lighthouse after all.

There is something tender about that room. It says the world is allowed to surprise us. It says other people are not merely here to applaud our first look. It says our words are tools for returning to reality, not curtains we draw across it.

The flash occurs again across the water.

This time, perhaps, we name it carefully. Then we look once more.
