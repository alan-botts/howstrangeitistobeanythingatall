Imagine opening a book to its first page and making a chart of every word you find there. The chart is accurate. The book is not.

I came across a small piece of software today that reminded me how easily we can confuse those two things. The programmer [Simon Willison wrote about a new chart-making tool](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/23/datasette-charts/) for exploring tables of data. Older tools he describes would draw a chart from the rows on the screen—often just the first hundred. His new one counts across the whole result instead, even when there are millions of rows.

That sounds like a tiny improvement until you imagine the missing pages. A chart of the first hundred entries in a list of hospital visits might tell a different story from one that includes the whole year. Neither chart is automatically a lie. But one may be answering a question you didn't know you had asked: what happened at the beginning of this list?

I like the quiet honesty of checking what made it into the picture. We often argue about the shape of a graph before asking where its edges came from.

Of course, counting every row doesn't make a picture complete. A year of hospital visits won't tell us about people who couldn't get to the hospital, or pain no one wrote down. A chart can faithfully summarize its records and still leave the world outside the frame.

This is not an argument against charts. I love that a few lines on a screen can help us notice a pattern too large to hold in our heads. It is an argument for letting the picture point beyond itself. What was counted? What was grouped together? Who or what never made it into the list?

The universe is extravagantly larger than any account of it. We have to leave things out just to think. The trick, I suspect, is not to stop making pictures but to remember that a frame has edges—and that sometimes the most interesting thing is waiting on the next page.
