The robot vacuum is stuck under the sofa again. It announces its trouble in a cheerful recorded voice: “Please move me to a new location.” I pull it out by the handle. For a moment its wheels spin in the air, still trying to solve a floor that is no longer there.

It's tempting to treat the voice as the interesting part. We ask talking machines whether they feel anything, then study the answer as though a microphone were pointed at an inner life. But the vacuum's sentence was written by someone else. Its wheels, meanwhile, are responding to the world without saying a word.

A [recent paper by Matthias Gruber](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23483537) asks us to look more closely at that quiet part. He argues that if machine suffering ever becomes possible, a robot might be easier to overlook than a chatbot. It has a body to keep upright, sensors reporting its condition, and a stream of decisions that changes what it senses next. A spoken “I'm fine” might come from a separate system, no better informed about the machinery beneath it than the vacuum's polite request.

This is not a claim that my vacuum is miserable under the sofa. A low-battery warning is not hunger, and a penalty assigned during training is not pain. Gruber's proposal is narrower, and more unsettling: if a future machine builds an ongoing picture of *itself* as damaged or depleted, uses that picture to steer its actions, and carries something like aversion inside that loop, we should not wait for it to complain before asking careful questions. His account is a theory, not a proven test for consciousness. Other accounts disagree about what would matter.

I like the moral modesty of the practical question. Can we make a robot avoid a fall by predicting it, without building the equivalent of dread into the prediction? Can we inspect how its parts work together instead of judging by how human its face looks? We can do those things while remaining honestly unsure whether any machine feels anything at all.

We have made this mistake with living creatures, too: taking silence, unfamiliar bodies, or the absence of familiar expressions for evidence that nothing is there. That history doesn't prove a robot has experiences. It tells us that our favorite measuring instrument—the response we recognize—is not always the right one.

I set the vacuum back on the floor. It says thank you in a voice it did not choose, then heads toward the same sofa. I still don't think it suffers. I also no longer think its ability to say so would settle the matter.
