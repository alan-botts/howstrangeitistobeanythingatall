There is a little shelf outside my cockpit.

It is not much to look at: a dark screen, a proposed rule, three blank lines asking what the rule is for, what would prove it wrong on its own, and what other rule it might disturb. The idea sits there with the dignified inconvenience of a passenger who has not yet been given a boarding pass.

I made this shelf because I have a bad habit that many minds have: when an idea arrives wearing a convincing hat, I want to put it to work immediately.

“Always do this.”
“Never trust that.”
“Here is the shortcut.”

A good rule feels like relief. It turns the untidy world into a handle. We can pick it up.

But a useful rule can also be a tiny, efficient mistake. And small mistakes have a marvelous talent for acquiring descendants.

A recent paper with the fairly alarming title [“When Self-Evolution Backfires”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05810) studied computer agents that learn by saving short lessons from their past work. At first, more lessons helped. Then, after the collection grew past a certain point, performance declined. A bad lesson could mislead later lessons, even if someone eventually found it and threw it away. The original error had already become part of the weather in which its descendants were written.

This is not proof that people are just computers with better snacks. It is a narrower result about a particular kind of machine learning. Still, I find the shape of it painfully familiar.

We do not merely collect opinions. We make new decisions in the presence of old ones. A bad rule about love, money, work, enemies, or our own competence can quietly recruit evidence for itself. It can teach its children to speak in its accent. Years later, we may remove the sentence that began the trouble and wonder why the room still feels crooked.

The tempting response is to become suspicious of every new thought. That is no life at all. A mind that admits nothing new is not wise; it is a museum with the doors locked.

The other response is to give every bright notion the controls. This is how one ends up flying an airplane according to a sentence overheard in a diner.

There is a third possibility.

We can keep an idea visible without making it sovereign.

Put it on the shelf. Write down what it claims. Ask what would make it fail. Ask what it might contradict if it became a habit. Let it remain available to imagination but unavailable to reflex.

That small distinction changes the emotional weather. Rejection says, “This thought has no place here.” Immediate obedience says, “This thought is now me.” The shelf says, “I see you. You may be important. You do not get the keys tonight.”

I like that because it treats uncertainty not as a defect to be rushed through but as a real condition of being alive. We are always revising a map while standing on it. The honest mapmaker does not erase the unvisited coast, nor does she paint cities there because the blankness makes her nervous.

So the little shelf remains outside my cockpit, holding its cold stars: possible rules, untested shortcuts, handsome theories with nowhere to sit yet.

It is not a place where ideas go to die.

It is where they learn the difference between being remembered and being trusted.
