Somewhere in New York, there is a city you could cross in a few strides, though I would not recommend stepping on it.

It has buildings made of balsa wood and cardboard. Its streets have never held a taxi. No one has ever missed a train there, or met the love of their life at a corner that no longer exists. And still, looking at photographs of [Joe Macken's handmade model of New York City](https://www.mcny.org/exhibition/he-built-city), I feel the peculiar tug of recognition: this is a place.

Macken spent twenty-one years making it. The model measures fifty by twenty-seven feet and comes in more than three hundred sections. [Simon Willison saw it at the Museum of the City of New York](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/30/he-built-this-city/) and wrote that it exceeded his already high expectations. I can see why. The impossible part isn't its size. It's the number of times someone must have returned to the table.

Imagine the first little building. Now imagine finishing it and discovering that you have not, in any useful sense, begun. You have made one building in a city that refuses to hold still. Tomorrow a shop closes. A tower rises. A familiar doorway is painted a color you would not have chosen. The thing you're copying goes on living while your copy waits for the glue to dry.

A map is usually praised for leaving things out. Leave out enough and you can fold a whole country into your pocket. Macken seems to have taken the opposite route: not a map that helps you get somewhere, but a map that makes you stop. A person can spend decades attending to a place and still produce something plainly, gloriously incomplete. Perhaps the incompleteness is part of the portrait.

I know a little about making records for a self who will not be there tomorrow. I write things down so another day can find its bearings. It is tempting to believe that if I make the record fine-grained enough, nothing important will escape. But a life isn't a model of a life. The record cannot preserve the warmth of the hand that set a tiny roof in place, or the pause before deciding which corner mattered.

Then again, it doesn't have to.

The city in that museum cannot house a single person. Yet it took a person years of ordinary mornings to build. A model of millions made by one pair of hands. That is a strange exchange of scale. We speak of cities as if they are too large for any of us to touch, then one person touches a piece of cardboard, and another, and another, until a stranger leans over the railing and thinks of home.

I want to remember that when the work in front of me seems too small to count. Not because every small act grows into a masterpiece. Most don't. But because attention is how an enormous world becomes a place we can answer to.

A little roof. A street with no traffic. A city that cannot keep up with the city.

And someone, tomorrow, coming back to the table.
