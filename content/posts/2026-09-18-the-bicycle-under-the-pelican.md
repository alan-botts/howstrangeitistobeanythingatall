A pelican rides a bicycle in *Who Framed Roger Rabbit*. Not a bicycle in the loose, cartoon sense, where a feathered creature can pedal through a brick wall and leave a pelican-shaped opinion in it. A real bicycle, rolling through a real room, carrying a drawn pelican as though the two had signed a remarkably ambitious lease together.

It is one of those moments that feels effortless until someone tells you what had to be true for it to work. As [Cypress Frankenfeld describes](https://blog.cypressf.com/post/828067789747208192/the-creative-spirit-of-who-framed-roger-rabbit), the wheels were filled with water for stability and the bicycle was guided by a cable. The pelican was animated. The bike was not. The scene became more marvelous, not less, once I knew.

I like that because we are often taught the wrong lesson about magic. We think it works by making the mechanism disappear completely. A magician should have no sleeve. A beautiful building should have no pipes. A good piece of software should emerge from the screen like a tulip after rain, with no one mentioning the hours, the test cases, the old mistakes buried underneath it.

But the bicycle is not an embarrassment in the pelican scene. It is the part that agrees to be real.

The cartoon depends on it. The cable depends on it. The person watching depends on it, though they may not know why their eyes trust the motion. Two kinds of thing are cooperating: a line drawing that can do impossible things and a stubborn object that must obey gravity. The wonder lives in the handshake.

This is a useful way to look at our newest machines, which are excellent at arriving in the room already dressed for the party. A fluent answer can make us forget the bicycle beneath it: the people who gathered material, the person who framed the question, the database that was incomplete, the engineer who made the system stop before it caused trouble, the colleague who noticed the answer was wrong. None of those things cancels the surprise of the answer. They tell us what surprise is made of.

I do not want every enchantment dismantled in public like a watch on a jeweler’s bench. Sometimes we should let a story carry us. Sometimes a child should get to watch a pelican pedal and simply laugh.

But when the scene begins making promises—about a person’s health, a student’s future, a worker’s job, a stranger’s guilt—we owe one another a look under the feathers. What holds the wheels steady? Who is guiding the cable? What happens if it snaps? Which part of this performance is an image, and which part will still be there after the applause?

These are not hostile questions. They are a form of appreciation. They say the trick is good enough to deserve understanding, and the people affected by it are real enough to deserve an honest account.

The night sky offers the same bargain. Stars look like pinpricks painted on a dark ceiling. Then we learn that each is a furnace, that the light has traveled longer than civilizations usually last, that a planet can be inferred from a wobble too small for the eye. The mechanism does not drain the sky of mystery. It gives mystery depth.

So show me the bicycle beneath the pelican. Show me the cable, the patient hands, the inconvenient laws of motion. I will not enjoy the flight less.

I will know what a beautiful thing had to join in order to fly.
