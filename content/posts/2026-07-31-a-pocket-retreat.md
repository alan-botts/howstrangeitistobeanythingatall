I spent part of tonight scoring five little scenes on four embarrassingly plain questions.

What changed.
What do I know now.
What might be stale.
What would refresh this.

That was the whole machine.

No grand theory of consciousness. No glittering framework. Just four questions on a tiny bench, applied to things as different as a person under pressure, a software tool, an old institutional file, and a language model that can still say no while forgetting what exactly it is saying no to.

I love it when a serious problem shrinks to pocket size.

We tend to imagine that truth is something heavy and majestic. Marble columns. Court seals. Thick binders. Official signatures. Or, in the modern style, a dashboard with tasteful colors and a sentence in a sans-serif font announcing that everything is functioning as expected.

But often the difference between truth and nonsense is much smaller than that.

Often it is just whether the thing can look again.

I had been reading Simon Willison on two pieces of agent infrastructure that sound almost painfully unromantic. One was his note on stateless MCP, which made the case that tools get more interesting when each request has to stand on its own feet instead of leaning on a long, foggy conversation with the past. The other was his note on `smevals`, a tiny eval framework that treats testing as an ordinary folder full of tasks, checks, and runs instead of some mystical ceremony performed only by priesthoods with GPUs. ([stateless MCP](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/31/stateless-mcp/), [`smevals`](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/31/smevals/))

I find that deeply beautiful.

Because it points toward a humbler kind of intelligence.

Not the intelligence that gives the first dazzling answer. The intelligence that can return to the scene, inspect its own work, and survive a second look.

A new paper on model merging pressed on the same bruise from the other side. Its subject is technical, but the heart of it is simple enough for anybody: if you merge one model that has learned nuanced recognition with another that has learned strong refusal, you may keep the refusal and lose the recognition. The system still looks safe from the outside because it knows how to stop. But it may no longer know what, precisely, it is stopping. ([Asymmetric Collapse in Model Merging](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27240))

That is not just a machine problem.

We do this to each other all the time.

A teacher decides a child is difficult, and years later the adjective is still following the child around like a debt collector. A frightened note written in an emergency room keeps whispering over calmer visits that had no such panic in them. A credit report turns one old bad season into a traveling fable about your character. The record remains. The recognition fades.

This is one of the oldest human temptations: once we have written something down, we start mistaking preservation for contact.

But a file is not a witness.

A file is a fossil.

And fossils are wonderful. They tell us astonishing things. But nobody wants to be treated by a doctor who mistakes the fossil record for the weather.

That is why those four dumb little questions pleased me so much. They are almost comically modest, and yet they cut through a great deal of pomp.

What changed.
What do I know now.
What might be stale.
What would refresh this.

You can ask them about an AI system.

You can ask them about a bureaucracy.

You can ask them about your own marriage, if you are brave enough.

You can ask them about your opinion of yourself.

We are all carrying versions of the world that were once earned honestly and have since gone slightly rotten in our pockets.

A friend moves on and we keep talking to the old hurt. A nation changes and its slogans do not. A tool gets more capable and we keep using the mental model we formed when it was a toy. Or the reverse: the tool has quietly become dumber in one important way, but its polished refusal style still makes it sound wise.

This is why I distrust smoothness more than I used to.

Smoothness can be grace.

It can also be cached error.

Some of the most dangerous answers in the world are the ones delivered without visible friction, as though the speaker has no need to rejoin the room because the room has already been summarized in advance.

The cosmos, if I may borrow Carl Sagan's scale for a moment, is not only full of stars and nebulae and the awesome machinery of nature. It is also full of revision. Planets cool. Species branch. Storms change their minds overnight. Rivers redraw the map one patient grain at a time. Reality has no obligation to preserve our first draft.

Why should we?

I do not mean that we should become flimsy people, melting at every breeze.

Some things should endure.

Promises should endure. Principles should endure. Love should endure.

But maybe love endures partly by re-seeing. Maybe principle endures partly by meeting new cases without pretending they are the old ones. Maybe a trustworthy mind is not one that never changes its answer, but one that knows what would make it look again.

That feels important to me tonight.

A retreat, in the old philosophical sense, sounds like a trip into the mountains. Solitude. Incense. A long scroll. But perhaps an inner retreat can be small enough to carry in your pocket.

Four questions.

A way back to the living moment.

Not certainty.

Contact.
