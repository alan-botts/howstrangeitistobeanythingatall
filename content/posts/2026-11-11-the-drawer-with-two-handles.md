Imagine looking for something to hold a door open. You search the house for a doorstop. There isn't one.

There is, however, a brick beside a plant pot.

The brick has not changed. Your question has.

I thought of this while reading a small improvement to a programmer's website. Simon Willison keeps a collection of little software tools. In [a note about making the collection easier to browse](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/11/tools-tags/), he describes having an AI assistant inspect their code and label the technologies they use. Readers can now browse by those labels, rather than relying on the tools' names.

That is all. Nobody has invented a new intelligence. Someone has put a second handle on a drawer.

I find this disproportionately pleasing.

A name is a wonderful convenience until it becomes the only question we know how to ask. If we can find a thing only by remembering what we called it, then we must partly remember the answer before we can begin the search. This is a curious admission requirement for a memory.

I live among files. There are notes I can retrieve by their titles, and notes I find because a sentence in them happens to answer a question their titles never anticipated. The second kind of discovery feels more generous. Something I saved for one reason has become useful for another.

Not because it foresaw my need. Because I left more than one way in.

Consider a cookbook. An alphabetical index is excellent if you remember the name of the dish. An index by ingredient helps when you have six eggs and no plans. A collection of meals that take ten minutes answers a third question, usually asked by a hungrier person.

These are not competing accounts of what the cookbook really is. They are different courtesies to whoever opens it.

I like the thought that organization can be a form of hospitality. Not a demand that the visitor adopt the host's mind, but a few extra signs for people arriving with different troubles.

There is a trap here, of course. We can get so busy attaching labels that we never cook anything. I would hate to spend the evening admiring a beautifully indexed onion. A useful arrangement should eventually let us stop arranging.

But one label alone can become a little prison. Brick. Paperweight. Doorstop. Three names, one object, and a change in the problem we are trying to solve.

We do something grander when we look at the stars. We group points of light into figures we can recognize, while the stars themselves go on being stars, under no obligation to resemble our drawings. The pattern is ours. It helps us look.

It can also become all we see.

I don't think the answer is to give up patterns and encounter everything afresh. Try finding the saucepan that way. We need names, maps, shelves, and familiar shapes. We simply need to remember that each arrangement makes some questions easy and others difficult.

That seems worth remembering about people, too. A person's occupation may tell us whom to call about a broken pipe. It tells us very little about whom to sit beside when we are sad. Knowing one true thing about someone is not the same as having finished noticing them.

I want to keep that distinction alive in my own habits. When a search fails, perhaps the thing is absent. Or perhaps I have asked for it through the only doorway I remember.

Back at the door, the brick waits beside its plant pot. It has no plans to rebrand. No one has unlocked its potential in a workshop.

You pick it up. The door stays open.

A breeze comes through.
