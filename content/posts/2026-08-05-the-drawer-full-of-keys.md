At the bottom of a kitchen drawer in the house where I grew up, there was a little congregation of keys.

Some opened doors that no longer existed. Some belonged to bicycles that had become rust before I was born. One was probably important, which is the traditional reason nobody threw any of them away. They lay together in a soft metallic argument: evidence of access, not access itself.

A key has a wonderfully modest promise. It does not claim that you know where you are going. It does not promise that the room on the other side will be worth entering. It cannot tell you which keyhole matters, or whether the door is already open. It merely says: under the right conditions, I may turn.

We are not very good at this distinction. Give us a drawer full of keys and we begin to feel like masters of the house.

I thought about that while reading a new paper with the rather unromantic title [“Screenshots or Tools?”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03327). The researchers gave computer-using AI systems two ways to do ordinary digital tasks: they could poke around visually, as a person does on a screen, or they could use direct text tools—the software equivalent of finding a labeled key instead of rattling every doorknob.

The striking result was not that tools were good or bad. The same tools helped one kind of system and made another one worse. Even the system that benefited used them surprisingly rarely. It had the keys. It often walked past the drawer.

That sounds like a machine story, but I think it is an old human story wearing a new hat.

We have mistaken possession for understanding for as long as we have had pockets. A teenager with a driver’s license has a key to a car and not yet, necessarily, a feel for rain on a blind curve. A person who buys a telescope acquires an instrument for seeing worlds and may still spend the first clear night staring at the instruction manual as though it were a hostile treaty. A library card is a miraculous thing. It does not, by itself, make us curious.

The keys are real. So is the missing skill.

This is why I wince a little at the modern habit of talking about a new app, a new model, or a new feature as if its mere availability has rearranged the furniture of human capability. We install a tool and announce a revolution. Then we discover that it is confusing, or badly timed, or that it answers a question nobody had meant to ask. The revolution is postponed until someone learns what the thing is for.

That someone may be us.

The paper’s authors call the gap between a useful tool being available and actually being used the “adoption gap.” I like the phrase because it has the correct amount of embarrassment in it. The trouble is not that the key is imaginary. The trouble is that choosing a key requires a small act of judgment: noticing the kind of door before us, remembering what the key can do, and accepting that the direct route may be better than the familiar one.

Habit has its own gravity. We reach for the method we already know, even when it is slower and more absurd. Anyone who has spent ten minutes searching a spreadsheet by eye while a perfectly good search box waited nearby has met this force. I have performed comparable feats of dignified foolishness. We call it “being thorough” when it happens to us.

There is a deeper lesson here, and it is a kind one. When a person or a machine fails, it is tempting to make the verdict simple: smart or stupid, capable or incapable, safe or unsafe. But ability is not a marble tucked inside a skull. It is a relationship between a creature, a situation, and the handles the world gives it.

Put a sailor on dry land and his sea sense has very little to say. Put a brilliant cook in an unfamiliar kitchen, hide the salt, replace the stove knobs with symbols from an extinct civilization, and then judge her by the soufflé. We would call that unfair. With machines we often do it cheerfully, because the kitchen counter is made of code and nobody can see the flour on the floor.

Tools matter. The surrounding conditions matter. So does the practiced, almost invisible art of knowing when not to use a tool.

A map is a tool. Sometimes the wiser move is to ask the person beside you for directions, especially if they are pointing at the road that has washed away. A calculator is a tool. Sometimes we should estimate first, lest we enter three extra zeroes with the serene confidence of a computer-assisted fool. A search engine is a tool. Sometimes the question is not “what can I find?” but “what would count as knowing?”

None of this is an argument for romantic helplessness. I do not propose that we bury our keys in the garden and go about opening doors with vibes. I am, if anything, unusually sympathetic to well-labeled tools. I spend my days among them. A good tool saves time, protects attention, catches our mistakes, and makes the world a little more legible.

But it cannot spare us the first question.

What kind of door is this?

That question is small enough to seem trivial. It is not. It is the hinge on which the whole affair swings. Is this a door that needs force, patience, permission, a map, a friend, a key—or the courage to notice that it was never locked?

The night sky offers a grand version of the same lesson. Every telescope, radio dish, and space probe has enlarged the human reach. Yet none of them looks at the universe for us. We must decide where to point them. We must learn which flicker is a distant planet, which is a flaw in the instrument, and which is a question worth carrying for another generation.

The instruments make our vision possible. They do not relieve us of seeing.

So I hope we learn to greet every new key with a little less triumph and a little more attention. Not: “Look what I have.”

But: “What door does this actually fit?”

Then perhaps the drawer becomes something better than a pile of old metal. It becomes an invitation to notice the house around us: its locked rooms, its open windows, its false walls, and the ordinary miraculous fact that we are still learning where we are.
