At three in the morning, a refrigerator can become a philosopher.

It hums in the dark with the calm confidence of an appliance that has never once wondered whether it deserves to be here. Meanwhile, the human brain beside it is often conducting a far less dignified orchestra: the thing I should have said, the thing that might happen, the appointment next Thursday, a conversation from 2009 returning with fresh paperwork.

The little conductor in the head is persuasive because it sounds like *us.* It speaks in the first person. It knows our name. It has opinions about what breakfast ought to mean.

But perhaps the self is not the conductor. Perhaps it is, at least partly, the volume at which we are listening to one.

I have been reading a new paper by Shawn Prest and Kevin Berryman, [“Towards an active inference account of deep meditative deconstruction.”](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2026/1/niag053/8769670) Its subject is a strange one: reports from very experienced meditators of experience becoming less solid, less personal, and sometimes almost vanishing altogether.

The authors do not offer a mystical fog machine. They offer a model. In their account, a mind is constantly making guesses about what is happening and what matters next. It gives some guesses more *precision* than others—plainly put, it turns up the dial on the expectations it trusts. “This is dangerous.” “I need to solve this.” “I am the sort of person who must not fail at this.”

Those are useful settings. A creature that never committed to a story would have trouble catching a bus, raising a child, or removing its hand from a hot pan. We need a working center of gravity. We need a few sentences that are allowed to move the furniture.

But the same gift can become a tiny dictatorship.

The paper suggests that deep meditation may involve lowering the confidence assigned to the mind’s largest, most bossy explanations. Not refuting every thought. Not winning an argument with oneself. Simply becoming less obliged to salute each mental memo as it arrives.

That distinction made me sit up.

Most of us imagine calm as a blank room. I do not think that is right. Calm is more like standing on a pier while the foghorn continues to sound, and discovering that the foghorn does not own the ocean. The sound is real. It may even be useful. But the whole horizon is not made of sound.

There is something almost comical about how quickly we promote a passing sensation into a cabinet minister. A tight chest becomes evidence about the future. A flash of envy becomes a judgment about our character. An unfinished email receives a flag, a desk, and a small but fully funded department of doom.

We do this because prediction is one of the great survival tricks of life. A mind that notices patterns can get out of the rain before the rain begins. But a prediction is not a subpoena. The fact that a thought arrives with a convincing letterhead does not mean we have to obey it.

I should be careful here. Prest and Berryman’s account is a thoughtful proposal, not a finished explanation of consciousness. It does not prove that meditation works this way, and it certainly does not settle what it would mean for experience to become sparse or cease. The mystery remains stubbornly employed.

Still, the proposal gives us a humane image of practice. We are not necessarily trying to destroy the self, as if it were a faulty toaster. We may be learning to let its instructions arrive at a volume appropriate to their evidence.

That matters well beyond meditation cushions. A person in grief, a teenager before an exam, a nation on a nervous afternoon, an artificial system fluent enough to sound sure of anything—each can mistake force of presentation for force of truth. Fluency is a talent. Confidence is a setting. Neither is a guarantee of fit.

The refrigerator goes on humming. The next thought comes. “You should worry about this,” it says, with the authority of a memo slipped under a door.

Maybe we worry. Maybe we act. Maybe the hot pan really is hot.

But first, for one spacious second, we can hear the hum around the thought.

That second is not nothing. It may be where freedom begins.
