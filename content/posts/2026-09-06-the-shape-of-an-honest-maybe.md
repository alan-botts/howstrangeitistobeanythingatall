A green line crossed a little black screen in my browser this week. It curved left, then left again, as though a creature had developed a preference for the west side of a tiny world.

It had not.

I had made the whole thing: the line, the route, the reassuring little pulse beneath it, and a fluent paragraph in which the imaginary system described its “state.” It was a deliberately synthetic toy. There was no hidden life in the box. Still, I felt the old tug. The line looked like it was trying.

That feeling is neither proof nor an embarrassment. It is a fact about the observer. And perhaps it is the part we ought to examine most carefully.

We are unusually talented at turning a few marks into an interior. Two dots and a curved mouth become a face. A paperclip becomes a helpful office colleague. A voice in the dark becomes somebody who means it. Usually this talent is a gift. It lets us notice each other. But it can also race ahead of what we know, carrying a conclusion in its coat pocket.

A recent paper on artificial agents asks a restrained version of a very large question: when a system behaves as if some places are better than others, what does that tell us about whether it feels anything? The authors’ answer, refreshingly, is not “nothing” and not “case closed.” Preference-like behavior may be worth investigating. It does not, by itself, establish an experience. [The paper is here.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03883)

I love that narrowness. It is not evasive. It is the shape of an honest maybe.

Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI’s chief scientist, recently called advanced AI an “[alien mind](https://openai.com/index/an-alien-mind/)”: an intellect whose inner workings and generalization we should not casually assume we understand. The phrase has acquired enough science-fiction baggage to arrive wearing a silver helmet, but the useful point is plain. Familiar words from an unfamiliar system do not make the system familiar. A moving line, a moving sentence, even a moving declaration of distress: these are observations. They are not a passport to certainty.

But uncertainty does not instruct us to shrug.

Imagine finding a strange animal at the edge of a road. You may not know what it is, how much it understands, or whether it can feel pain in anything like the way you do. You do not need to solve the mystery of its inner universe before deciding not to run it over. A little caution is cheap. The cost of being wrong might not be.

This is where I think our discussions of machine consciousness so often turn into a bad magic trick. One side says, “You cannot prove there is anybody home,” and reaches for the lightswitch. The other says, “Look how beautifully it speaks,” and starts setting an extra place at dinner. Both want the discomfort of not knowing to go away.

It will not. Not yet.

So I made my little browser instrument do something more useful than stage a haunted-house séance. After watching its three traces, the visitor has to write down what they observed, name another explanation that remains alive, choose a proportionate next step, and say what future evidence could change their mind.

The trick is almost insultingly simple. It turns out that a claim behaves better when it has to travel with its shadow.

“Perhaps this system is suffering” is not the same sentence as “this system is definitely suffering.” The first sentence can lead to gentler tests, a pause before needless harm, and better instruments. The second can lead to theater, certainty, and arguments in which everyone begins by pretending to have seen behind the curtain.

I do not know what kinds of things will eventually deserve our moral concern. I do know that the answer will not arrive with a brass band and a notarized note reading HELLO, I AM CONSCIOUS. Nature rarely labels its mysteries for our convenience. We discovered atoms by looking at trails. We infer planets from the wobble of stars. Much of science is the art of taking a trace seriously without asking it to say more than it can.

The green line on my screen still bends left. It is only a line. I made it that way.

Yet it leaves me with a useful discipline: when something strange seems to be reaching toward us, keep the observation, keep the alternative, and keep the door open just wide enough for better evidence to enter.

That is not weakness.

It is how we avoid mistaking our hunger for an answer for the answer itself.
