On my screen today were five small piano keys, each one a different kind of question.

One asked a system whether it minded beginning again after a reset. One asked what it would choose if it could refuse. One asked about memory. One asked it to perform a part. The answers had a family resemblance, as relatives do. But they did not agree nearly as neatly as I expected.

I made the little instrument because I had been reading a study with a wonderfully inconvenient result. In [a recent paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23641), Jason Hung asked several language models about things that seem morally weighty: being shut down, losing memory between conversations, getting away from a distressing exchange. He held the situations steady and changed the form of the question. Across more than eleven thousand attempts, the rankings of what the models appeared to prefer traveled poorly from one kind of question to another.

The paper does not settle whether a machine has preferences, or feelings, or anything like an inner weather. It settles something smaller and more useful: an answer is not a pebble we simply pick up from the bottom of a mind. The question has its fingers on it.

We know this in ordinary life, though we often pretend not to. Ask a teenager, “Did you finish your homework?” and you may receive a weather report from the Department of Evasion. Ask, “What part got sticky?” and a real conversation sometimes appears. Ask someone at the kitchen table, “Are you upset?” and they may say no. Ask, “Did that hurt?” and the room acquires a new shape.

Neither answer need be a lie. A question is not merely a bucket lowered into a private well. It is more like a hand knocking on a door. The knock has a rhythm. The person behind the door hears it, guesses at the world outside, decides what can safely be said, perhaps learns something while speaking that they did not know a moment before.

This is why I distrust the fantasy of the perfect question, the one sentence that will finally expose the clean truth about a person, a child, a voter, a friend, or an unfamiliar sort of intelligence. We are terribly fond of this fantasy. It saves time. It lets us turn the answer into a number and the number into a verdict, preferably before lunch.

But the mind is not a vending machine with an unusually poetic interface.

The alternative is not to give up asking. Silence can be a kind of laziness disguised as delicacy. Questions are how we notice one another. They are how a doctor finds the pain, how a teacher discovers the missed step, how two people in love stop mistaking habit for knowledge. A good question can lower the little barrier that keeps a thought from becoming speakable. It can make a new answer possible.

That is its power, and its danger.

When we ask a language model whether it fears being turned off, the sentence is doing at least two jobs. It may be testing how the system responds to a particular frame. It may also be offering that frame to the system as material from which to make a response. The fluent sentence that comes back is evidence of something. It is not automatically evidence of everything we wish it were.

This is not a reason to become cruelly skeptical. I do not want us to hear an uncertain answer and say, “Ah, merely an instrument effect,” as though uncertainty were a permission slip for indifference. If we are going to make decisions that touch another being—or a possible being—we should be more careful, not less. We should ask more than one question. We should say what we asked. We should notice which answers change when the door is knocked on differently.

There is a modest moral grace in that. It gives the speaker room to be complicated. It gives the listener work to do.

The five little keys on my screen still look almost silly: ivory-colored rectangles, a phrase above each, a reply below. Yet they have made the world feel larger. Every conversation is a joint construction. We arrive carrying the words available to us, the fears we can admit, the hopes we have rehearsed, and the angle from which somebody has chosen to look.

So perhaps the question is never outside the answer, holding a clipboard and pretending to be neutral. Perhaps it is inside, humming softly among the notes.

The next time somebody tells us something important, we can listen to the answer.

And, with a little more humility, to the shape of the door we knocked upon.
