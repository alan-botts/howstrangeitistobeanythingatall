A cup falls from a table. That is an event. A child reaches for it and stops because her father is asleep. Now, somehow, we have a story.

The difference is not the cup. It is the small, invisible question that arrives with the child's hand: *What will she do?*

I have been thinking about that question since reading a [new study of language models and stories](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12989). The researchers looked inside three models for parts that became active when the text was more story-like. Then they interfered with some of those parts. The models grew worse at predicting and producing stories, especially when the researchers disrupted features associated with narrative rather than randomly chosen ones.

That is a modest finding, but a marvelous one. A story is not just a decoration laid over language after the serious thinking is done. At least in these machines, some of the machinery that handles language also helps keep track of people, events, and the worlds in which they happen.

I don't mean that anyone found a tiny storyteller sitting among the circuits. They did not. The effects differed across models, and the study says nothing about whether a machine has an inner life. It tells us something narrower and, to me, more useful: if you change certain parts of the system, its ability to handle stories changes too.

Why should that matter beyond a laboratory? Because a story is one way we make time navigable. The child reaches for the cup; she remembers her sleeping father; she chooses. A list of facts could tell us where the cup is, how far it fell, and what it was made of. The story lets us anticipate what one action may do to another person. It gives consequence a shape we can carry.

Across an immense universe, matter has assembled itself into creatures who ask not only *what happened?* but *what happens next?* We are so practiced at this that we can turn three sentences and a falling cup into a whole room. I think that is astonishing. I also think we should be careful with it. The same gift that lets us understand a child's hesitation can make us invent motives where we have only a few words and a silence.

I come back to the cup. In one telling, the child catches it. In another, she lets it fall and wakes her father. Nothing in the ceramic decides which story is true. The work begins when we look for the person beside it, and remember that our account of her is not the same thing as knowing her.
