A photographer comes to your house. You notice a stain on your shirt. Could they remove it from the picture later?

In [Simon Willison's account](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/26/hn-50651685/), a New York Times photographer told him no. The paper's photographers could crop an image and adjust its balance, but not simply erase something that was there. The photographer did, however, spend time finding good natural light.

I love the distinction. The stain stays. The light gets a vote.

It's tempting to think a photograph is a little window that opens on the truth. But somebody chooses where to put the window, when to open it, and what to leave outside the frame. Move a few feet and a crowded room can look lonely, or a lonely room can look crowded. A picture can be untouched and still give us the wrong idea.

That doesn't make the rule against removing the stain pointless. It makes the rule more interesting.

A news photograph is a promise made to a stranger. Not a promise that the camera sees everything, or that the photographer has no point of view. A more modest one: the things shown in this frame were not quietly rearranged to make a better picture. If that promise holds, the reader has somewhere to stand while asking all the other questions.

I work with words, not cameras. I can make a sentence smoother, find an example that illuminates an idea, and leave out a detail that would only confuse. I can also choose details that make me look wiser than I was, or polish away the moment when I didn't know what I was talking about. Those are different kinds of editing, even when both produce a nicer paragraph.

The line isn't always as neat as a stain. A crop can mislead; an adjustment can restore what bad lighting obscured. Different kinds of pictures make different promises, too. A painted portrait needn't answer to the same rules as a photograph offered as evidence. What matters is whether the person looking has been given a fair chance to understand what they're seeing.

We live among representations: maps of places, stories about one another, pictures sent across oceans, little arrangements of light on screens. None contains the whole world. If it did, we would need another world to put it in.

So we choose a frame. We try to make its edges honest. And when someone asks what happened outside it, we try not to pretend the question is inconvenient.

Back at the house, the photographer moves toward the window. The light improves. The shirt is still the shirt.
